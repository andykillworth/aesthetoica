On the word
æsthetoica
with some account of the ledgers of Thomasin Gale, bookbinder, of Broad Street, Lyme Regis
Printed for the tray, and sold nowhere

Thomasin Gale bound books in Broad Street, Lyme Regis, from 1811 until her eyesight went. Like most binders she made her own marbled endpapers, and like most marblers on the coast she made her size from carrageen, the purple moss that washes up below Church Cliffs after a south-westerly.

In the winter of 1823 she noticed that her tray was not keeping still.

She had left a bath of size overnight with a few drops of colour on it, as she sometimes did to test a pigment. In the morning the colours had moved. Not spread, which is expected, but travelled, in curls and stripes, as if something had been drawing in the tray while the house slept. She skimmed it, laid fresh colour, and the same thing happened the next night, and the next.

Gale did what a bookbinder does. She took a print every morning and kept a ledger. Her entries are short and practical, and a few of them are given below.

She needed a word for the sheets and could not find one, so she made one. She knew enough Greek from her Bible-binding trade to know that aisthētikos means a thing that can be perceived. Her sheets were the opposite: things that passed through the room and could not be felt. She added an ending of her own and wrote it on the spine of the first ledger. Æsthetoica: what is felt by the tray and by nobody else.

She kept the tray going until 1839. On Christmas night of that year the cliffs at Bindon, a few miles west, slid into the sea in one of the largest landslips in English history. Gale had moved the tray to her sister’s cottage on that stretch of coast for the holiday. The cottage went with the land. So did the tray.

The ledgers survived, eleven volumes and a little over five thousand sheets. People who have studied them since note two things. The ochre is heaviest in the years when the Sun is quietest. And the combing happens more than once.

The tray was never found. This is an attempt to keep the sheets going, numbered on from hers.

From the first and fourth ledgers

3 Dec 1823Left a test bath. Colours moved in the night. Skimmed it.
4 DecThe same. Took a print and kept it.
11 Jan 1824Ochre fine, like rain through a roof.
2 FebGreen turning the whole tray, strong. Mr Hounsell says there were lights in the north.
19 MarBlue in drops, unaccountable.
13 Nov 1833Red lines all night, very quick, too many to count.
7 Mar 1836Combed. No one here.
22 Dec 1839Taking the tray to Susan’s for Christmas.

13 November 1833. That night the great Leonid meteor storm was seen across North America.

The entries are her own; the spelling has been modernised. The blots of colour are where the page touched the tray.

Thomasin Gale, her tray and her ledgers are a fiction. Carrageen marbling size, the cliffs below Church Cliffs and the Bindon landslip of Christmas 1839 are not.
